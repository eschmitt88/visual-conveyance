Fernwick Search Transit: four lines leave the prototype — which one do we ride?

Choosing the production vector-search engine behind semantic product search, drawn as a transit map. Four candidate lines depart; the must-have fare-control wall cuts two; the surviving two trade fares against operations — and the ticket is conditional, with the decision made at the 2026-10-01 checkpoint.

The five gates (must-haves — miss any one and you're out, regardless of other merits)

M1 p95 latency ≤ 100 ms at 10M vectors under the production mix, including the 30% filtered traffic (the search page has 250 ms end-to-end; vector search gets 100 ms; p95 = the time even the slowest 1-in-20 queries stay under) · M2 recall@10 ≥ 0.92 on unfiltered queries vs exact brute-force ground truth (recall@10 = how many of the true top-10 results the engine actually finds) · M3 metadata filtering with recall@10 ≥ 0.90 down to 5% filter selectivity (a filter matching only ~1 in 20 products) — post-hoc filtering that silently drops recall does not count · M4 all product and query data stays in an EU region · M5 ≤ $2,500/month run cost at the 12-month scale (~10M vectors).

Reading this map

  2. Every line must pass the fare-control wall: the five must-have gates. Two lines end there; the red tags say exactly which gate cut them.
  3. Past the wall, follow the two survivors rightward. Horizontal distance is corpus size; vertical height is monthly infrastructure fare. Watch the blue line start cheapest, cross the amber line around 3M vectors, and end 3.4× more expensive at 100M.
  4. The ◎ interchange plus the ticket-rules banner is the actual decision. It is an IF, not a winner: which line Fernwick rides is settled at the 2026-10-01 checkpoint by two headcount conditions, with one standing override.

Stop 1 — the fare-control wall: who got cut, and why it isn't taste

All four candidates ran the same two-week bakeoff (2026-06-08 → 2026-06-19): one frozen 10M-vector snapshot (the 4.1M live corpus plus 5.9M synthetic-but-realistic products; 10M is the scale the decision must hold at), identical hardware for the self-hosted options (up to 3 nodes of 16 vCPU / 64 GB / NVMe), a 48-hour production query log replayed at 120 QPS with the live 30% filter mix, and recall@10 measured against exact brute-force ground truth on 10,000 sampled queries. Quiver was measured from inside the VPC via private peering; its ~2 ms network hop is included in its numbers.

Braid (embedded library) — cut at gate M3. The fastest, highest-recall thing measured: 12 ms p95 unfiltered, 0.98 recall@10, a 3-minute full index build at 60,000 vectors/s, and the cheapest infrastructure at every scale. None of it matters: Braid has no native filtered search, so filtering is post-hoc and recall collapses as filters get selective — 0.94 at 50% selectivity but 0.71 at 5%, far below M3's 0.90 floor. This is structural (the index doesn't know about metadata), not tunable. Building a filtering layer plus DIY serving, snapshots, and replication would cost ~1.5 FTE for two quarters — and its ~$4,200/mo 100M estimate excludes that self-built sharding layer (~2 engineer-quarters). This is the answer to "but the library benchmarks best": it fails a must-have.
pgnearest (Postgres extension, v0.9) — cut at gates M1 and M2. Filtered latency is 110 ms p95 — over the 100 ms budget (unfiltered scrapes by at 95 ms) — and recall@10 is 0.89 both ways, under M2's 0.92 and also under M3's 0.90. The recall is an index-architecture ceiling, not a tuning problem. Attractive otherwise: plain SQL, lowest marginal ops (~0.1 FTE, it rides the existing Postgres fleet), minimal lock-in — but it's a young project (two years, effectively one core maintainer). Signal S5: if the undated v1.0 actually ships recall parity, a ≈1-week re-benchmark reopens this line; waiting for it is not.

Stop 3 — the fare zones: where "cheap" flips

Two crossovers, pointing in different directions:

  • Infra-only (≈3M vectors — already behind us). Quiver is cheapest at 1M ($180 vs Hearth's $520 HA floor), but the curves cross around 3M. At today's 4.1M the quote is already $740 vs $520; the gap widens to $1,900 vs $1,400 at 10M and $23,000 vs $6,800 — 3.4× — at 100M. On infrastructure alone, self-hosting is the cheaper option today.
  • Labor-adjusted (≈30M vectors, ~2029 at 2×/yr growth). Hearth's ~0.2 FTE steady-state is ≈ $2,800/mo of loaded engineer cost. Counting it, Quiver stays cheaper in total until roughly 30M vectors. So "the SaaS is cheaper" is right until ~2029 — if that labor is truly marginal. It is not, if the PLAT-7 hire happens anyway for broader platform duties.

The cost argument for Hearth is therefore a bet on Fernwick's own growth curve: at 2×/yr the corpus reaches the labor-adjusted crossover in ~3 years, and every year on Quiver past that point costs more and deepens lock-in. (The 100M-scale fares for the eliminated lines were ~$13,500/mo estimated for pgnearest — on an unvalidated 4-shard fleet whose projected latency fails anyway — and ~$4,200/mo for Braid excluding the serving layer it lacks.)

Stop 4 — the interchange: a conditional ticket, not a winner

Primary: Hearth (self-hosted), contingent on BOTH holding at the 2026-10-01 checkpoint:

  • the approved platform-infra hire (req PLAT-7) has signed and started by 2026-10-01 — the req is approved but not yet filled, and the whole plan leans on it;
  • a ≥4-engineer on-call rotation for the search-infra service is committed by the Platform team, in writing, by the same date.

Fallback: if either fails, Quiver — with a mandatory exit plan, not an open-ended commitment: all query construction stays behind the internal SearchIndex interface (no Quiver-proprietary DSL outside the adapter); a quarterly export drill (full JSONL export restored into a scratch Hearth cluster, timed and logged); a standing ~3 engineer-week migration budget re-estimated at each renewal; re-evaluation at 30M vectors or the first contract renewal, whichever comes first.

Standing override (S1), beating both: if filtered queries exceed 60% of traffic or common facet selectivity drops below ~2%, the recommendation becomes Quiver even if the hire lands — Hearth's filtered recall (0.90 at the 0.90 floor, 0.86 at 1%) has no margin there, while Quiver holds 0.95 at 1%. Filter share has grown every quarter; this is the trigger to watch.

Why Hearth first and not the reverse: at the current growth rate the labor-adjusted crossover (~30M, ~2029) falls inside the lifetime of this decision; lock-in on Quiver compounds with every proprietary feature adopted; and in-VPC residency (N1) is plausibly a hard requirement for 2027 enterprise-seller contracts — a door Quiver keeps closed permanently. Quiver is the better engine today on almost every soft criterion; Hearth is the better position to be standing in come 2029 — reachable only with the headcount to hold it. Dates: headcount checkpoint 2026-10-01; full decision review 2027-01-15 (re-check the filter-share trend, PLAT-7 status, Quiver renewal posture, pgnearest v1.0).

Signals along the track — what flips the decision

  • S1 — filters become primary → flips to Quiver, regardless of headcount (the standing override above).
  • S2 — the ops headcount doesn't materialize → flips to Quiver. Hearth without PLAT-7 puts on-call and tuning on four product engineers who already own the search stack — the cost advantage becomes unbudgeted attrition risk.
  • S3 — growth accelerates to 3×/yr → strengthens Hearth. 100M arrives in ~2.5 years and the labor-adjusted crossover moves under 2 years out; Quiver's Enterprise pricing dominates the picture.
  • S4 — Quiver renewal repricing → strengthens Hearth. The quote is locked for 12 months only; a 25% renewal increase (their published list moved ~20% last year) moves the labor-adjusted crossover from ~30M to ~20M vectors. This is why the fallback carries a mandatory exit plan.
  • S5 — pgnearest v1.0 ships recall parity → reopens the cheapest low-ops line. Worth a 1-week re-benchmark when it exists; not worth waiting for on an undated roadmap.

Where the metaphor breaks

On a real transit map, transfers are free and the geometry is permanent. Neither is true here. Transferring between these lines is a migration — leaving Quiver costs ~3 engineer-weeks today and more with every proprietary feature adopted; that asymmetry is exactly why the Quiver fallback ships with a pre-paid "return ticket" (adapter boundary, export drills, standing migration budget) instead of an open-ended ride. And the map's fares are not fixed track: Quiver's pricing is locked for only 12 months (S4), and pgnearest's cut could be reversed by a v1.0 that doesn't exist yet (S5). Finally, a rider chooses how far to travel; Fernwick doesn't — the corpus doubles every year on its own, carrying the company rightward into the expensive zones whether or not anyone buys a ticket.
