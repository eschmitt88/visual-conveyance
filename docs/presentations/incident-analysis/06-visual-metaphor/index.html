The June 24 flood: one storm, a silted riverbed, and pumps that fed the water back in

A 4-hour-17-minute partial outage, mapped as a river basin. There was no single root cause: a bad deploy was the storm, but it took two pre-existing weaknesses — silt in the riverbed and jitterless retry pumps — to turn one endpoint's regression into a valley-wide flood. Remove any one of the three and June 24 is a slow day, not an outage. The P0 fixes (Q3 2026) target one leg each.

Reading this map

  1. Follow the water left to right. The search stream starts under the storm, crosses the silted flats, and joins every other endpoint's traffic at the one shared canal before reaching the customer districts.
  2. The flood needed all three weaknesses. The storm (①) is the trigger; the silt (②) and the pumps (③) are amplifiers that already existed. Remove any single one and the flood shrinks to a one-endpoint degradation — or nothing at all.
  3. Numbered markers ① – ⑥ on the map key the sections below. All times are UTC, June 24 2026, unless dated otherwise.
  4. The review is blameless. It names systems, tools, and process gaps — not people. Every decision made during the incident was reasonable given what responders could see at the time.

1The storm — a deploy that turned drops into torrents

Release 2026-06-24.3 reached 100% at 14:02, carrying PR #8841: invoice search upgraded from prefix matching (“starts with”) to contains matching (“anywhere in the number”) — a long-requested feature. Two things changed at once in the ORM layer:

  • The pattern gained a leading wildcard: invoice_number LIKE $1 || '%' became invoice_number LIKE '%' || $1 || '%'.
  • Org scoping moved out of the SQL WHERE clause into application-side post-filtering, so the query ran unscoped across all organizations. Results were identical after filtering — which is exactly why the change passed review and its behavioral tests.
Why that is catastrophic in Postgres: a B-tree index can serve a prefix pattern (it's a range scan), but a leading wildcard gives the index nothing to anchor on — Postgres cannot use it at all. With the org filter gone from SQL too, the planner's only option was a sequential scan: read the entire 274-million-row table for every single search.

The evidence, from captured query plans. Before: Index Only Scan using idx_invoices_org_number (cost=0.56..8.44) — mean 0.4 ms, p99 2.1 ms; the index covered the query, so the heap was never touched. After: Seq Scan on invoices (cost=0.00..6,214,891), Rows Removed by Filter: 312,344,806, Buffers: shared hit=3,214,556 read=24,661,020 — actual time 24–31 seconds per query, the whole table: 274.2 M live rows plus 38.1 M dead tuples, ≈213 GB of heap.

Two mercies kept it from being worse: Postgres synchronized concurrent sequential scans (parallel searches shared one pass over the table), and the heap was partially cached. Without those, scans would have taken minutes, not tens of seconds.

Why canary didn't catch it: the canary invoices table holds ~2 M rows; the same seq scan there takes ~180 ms — slow, but under every alert threshold. The regression was invisible until the query met production-scale data.

2The silted flats — a riverbed raised two days earlier

Gloss: in Postgres, an UPDATE doesn't overwrite a row — it writes a new version and leaves the old one behind as a “dead tuple.” A background process, autovacuum, reclaims dead tuples. Until it runs, the table is physically larger (“bloat”), and every full-table scan pays to read the corpses.

On June 22 — two days before the incident — a backfill populated the new tax_region column on 38.1 M historical invoices. That single job left 38.1 M dead tuples, growing the heap from 190 GB to 213 GB (+12%); dead tuples were 38.1 M of 312.3 M total — 12.2% bloat.

Why autovacuum never ran: it triggers when dead tuples exceed 20% of live rows (the default scale factor). On a 274.2-million-row table that threshold is ≈54.9 M dead tuples; the backfill's 38.1 M fell short, so autovacuum — correctly, by its configuration — did nothing. The default is sized for small tables; on very large ones it permits tens of gigabytes of bloat. ANALYZE (statistics refresh) likewise never triggered.

Why the silt mattered: alone, it was harmless — index-backed queries skip dead tuples cheaply, and nothing scans this table in normal operation. But once the storm turned every search into a full-table scan, each scan read 312.3 M tuples instead of 274.2 M, and ~23 GB of extra heap. That pushed scan times from an estimated ~26 s (bloat-free) to the observed 24–31 s — straddling the 30-second levee. During the incident, ~57% of search queries were killed by the statement timeout (query_canceled), surfacing to clients as 5xx. The silt converted “slow” into “failing” — and a killed query still held its pooled connection for the full 30 seconds first.

3The retry pumps — clients that fed the flood back into the river

The SDKs and internal service clients retried 5xx responses on a fixed 1-second interval, 3 attempts, no jitter, no retry budget. Under sustained failure that is traffic multiplication: gateway inflow rose from a 3,200 rpm baseline to 15,020 rpm at peak — 4.7× — with visible 1-second-period oscillation as retry waves arrived in phase. The retries queued on the same exhausted pool, so the extra load produced zero extra successes; it only kept the canal pinned and the queue full for nearly two hours (15:12–17:18).

4The shared canal — how one stream's problem became everyone's

Gloss: the API doesn't open a Postgres connection per request; requests borrow one of a fixed pool of server connections managed by pgbouncer. If every connection is busy, new requests wait in a queue.

The arithmetic of exhaustion. The pool to the primary is sized at 80 connections, shared by invoice reads, writes, PDF rendering, and webhook dispatch. Pre-incident, search used a negligible share (1.2 searches/s × 0.4 ms each). After the deploy, each search held a connection for up to 30 s:

  • 14:07–15:00 — organic search traffic alone (≈1.2/s × ~28 s) occupied ~34 of 80 connections: heavily degraded, but the canal held.
  • Around 15:00, the day's largest cohort of hourly scheduled invoice-generation jobs landed, and retries multiplied search arrivals to ≈5.6/s. Demand hit ≈160+ concurrent connections against a pool of 80. Saturation at 15:12. From then on, every endpoint sharing the pool queued behind 30-second scans — invoice creation, PDF rendering, webhook dispatch. The blast radius stopped being “search.”
  • The client wait queue (cl_waiting) peaked at 1,437 at 15:58, and fully drained only at 17:18.

Why widening the canal backfired. At 16:20 the pool was raised 80 → 200 as an experiment. That admitted more concurrent sequential scans to the primary, worsening I/O contention and write p99; it was reverted at 16:31. The pool size was not the problem — the 30-second connection holds were.

How the flood assembled — and why any one missing leg changes everything

  1. The deploy made every search a 24–31 s full-table scan (the storm — the trigger).
  2. Backfill silt pushed those scans past the 30 s levee, so slow queries became failed queries (first amplifier).
  3. Failures triggered jitterless retries, multiplying search arrivals 4.7× (second amplifier).
  4. Multiplied 30-second connection holds exhausted the shared canal of 80, extending the failure to every endpoint behind it.
  5. Those endpoints' failures also retried, sustaining the storm until the sluice gate closed at 17:05 and removed the scans entirely.

No storm (no query change)

Nothing happens at all. The silt and the retry configuration had been individually harmless for months.

No silt (bloat-free table)

Scans run ≈26 s — painfully slow but mostly under the levee, so they succeed. Retries stay rare, the canal sits at ≈34/80, and June 24 is a one-endpoint degradation.

No pump storm (sane retries)

Demand stays near organic levels and the canal holds through the 15:00 job spike. The flood never leaves the search stream.

This is why the flood defenses (below) deliberately target all three legs rather than only the trigger.

5The wrong valley — 68 minutes of looking away from the river

68 minutes elapsed between the first alert (14:11) and the correct diagnosis (15:19) — of which 50 minutes (14:20–15:10) were spent actively pursuing a network hypothesis. Every gap was systemic:

  • The alert watched overflow, not water level. The only firing signal was “gateway 5xx > 2% over 5 min.” Search p99 exploded at 14:07 — four minutes before that alert — but no latency SLO alert existed for the invoicing endpoint group (checkout had one; invoicing did not).
  • Level readings sampled 1 in 100 requests. At 1.2 searches/s that is ~0.7 samples per minute — the search-latency panel looked like sparse noise, not a cliff, exactly when it mattered.
  • The deploy signpost was broken. The dashboard integration that annotates deploys had been broken since June 10 (a known ticket). The 14:02 deploy was invisible on every graph responders looked at — removing the single strongest hint.
  • A plausible distant storm arrived on cue. The cloud provider's 14:05 “elevated packet loss” advisory, plus gateway 504s that look like network timeouts, anchored the team on a network theory. A gateway/NAT failover at 14:38 brought no improvement; packet captures came back clean and the provider confirmed the zone was unaffected at 15:10. Given the missing deploy marker, the network theory was the most reasonable available explanation.

In retrospect, pg_stat_activity would have shown rows of identical sequential scans as early as ~14:15. Nothing routed responders toward the database until the canal's queue became impossible to miss at 15:15 (cl_waiting above 900 and climbing); at 15:19 pg_stat_activity showed dozens of concurrent seq scans from invoice search, and the incident commander pivoted.

6The bypass that couldn't be re-dug — and the gate that could close

Gloss: a covering index holds every column a query needs, so the query never touches the main table at all — the “bypass channel” that made old searches take 0.4 ms.

Release 2026-06-24.3 bundled migration 0412, which dropped idx_invoices_org_number — the covering index the old query depended on. The rationale was reasonable at review time: the new query shape no longer used it, and it cost 31 GB plus write overhead. But it made the deploy irreversible in practice:

  • Rolling back the code would restore the old prefix query — which now also has no index, and would seq-scan just the same.
  • Re-digging the bypass — recreating the index on 274 M rows — takes ~6 hours. (CREATE INDEX CONCURRENTLY was started at 16:05 as insurance; it finished at 21:48, well after incident close.)
  • Rolling forward with a query fix was estimated at 50+ minutes of build/deploy under storm conditions, with its own risk.

What worked: the sluice gate. The contains-search path had shipped behind the feature flag invoice_search_contains. After staging verification (16:35) that the exact-match fallback used the untouched unique index uq_invoices_org_number, the IC approved at 17:03 and the flag was set to 0% at 17:05. Errors fell below 3% within ten minutes; the canal's queue drained by 17:18. Rollback — the deploy pipeline — was not what stopped the bleeding; the flag was, and the flip itself took 90 seconds once approved.

The rest was cleanup: a manual VACUUM (ANALYZE) (17:12–17:50, cost limits disabled) dredged the 38.1 M dead tuples and refreshed statistics (disk space marked reusable, not returned — a pg_repack is scheduled separately), and the buffer caches that hours of 200-GB scans had evicted re-warmed. All SLOs were met from 17:53; the incident closed at 18:19 after 26 minutes of stable metrics. Search ran in degraded, exact-match-only mode until the index rebuild finished that evening; full contains search was re-enabled — with the fixed, org-scoped query — on June 26.

Notes on the numbers

  • Latency figures are for completed requests; requests that hit the 30-second gateway timeout surfaced as 504s and are counted as errors, not in the latency percentiles.
  • “Affected” means an organization saw at least one failed API request or dashboard action during the window. Endpoints served by read replicas (reporting) and the separately pooled auth service stayed on dry ground — which is why the platform-wide error rate peaked at 31.2% rather than higher.
  • Invoice writes were never lost: creation goes through a durable queue, and all 37,400 webhook deliveries queued during the outage were replayed to completion by 19:05 with zero drops. The blast radius was availability, not integrity.

What held

  • Feature-flag discipline paid for itself. Because the new query path shipped behind a flag, mitigation required no build, no deploy, and no schema change — the flip took 90 seconds once approved. On June 24, flags — not the deploy pipeline — were the working safety net.
  • No data was lost. Invoice writes ride a durable queue and webhook deliveries are queued with replay; no customer record was dropped or corrupted.

Flood defenses — nine action items, three per priority

The three P0 items all land in Q3 2026 and are tracked weekly at the ops review. Deliberately, each one reinforces a different leg of the flood: a water-level gauge for the valley (alert on latency, not just overflow), a canal alarm (pool-saturation alerting and isolation), and staggered pumps (retries with backoff and jitter).

Where the metaphor breaks

Three places this map would mislead if pushed:

  • Water is conserved; requests are not. Real floodwater has to come from somewhere. Here, the retry pumps created new flow out of nothing (clients resending), and the 30-second levee destroyed flow (killed queries). That is why more “water” produced zero extra deliveries downstream — the pumps added load, never throughput.
  • Widening a real canal relieves a flood. Widening this one (pool 80 → 200 at 16:20) made things worse, because the gates were never the constraint — the 30-second holds were, and more open gates only meant more simultaneous full-table scans grinding the primary. It was reverted in 11 minutes.
  • Silt in a real river slows everything, always. This silt was harmless for two days: index-backed queries step over dead tuples cheaply, and nothing normally scans the table. It only mattered once the storm forced every search to read the whole riverbed.

Lessons from the basin

  • Latent conditions are part of the system. The silt and the pump configuration were known-shaped risks, individually harmless for months. Outages of this class are conjunctions; a review that stops at the trigger fixes one third of the problem.
  • Alert on what the customer feels. Customers felt latency at 14:07; the first signal was an error-rate threshold at 14:11, and the dashboards couldn't show the latency cliff at all. Detection gaps cost more minutes than any technical failure on June 24.
  • Treat irreversible changes as a category. The engineers who bundled migration 0412 followed existing norms; the norms were wrong. Schema changes that foreclose rollback need their own release policy (action item 5), not more reviewer vigilance.
  • Retries without jitter are a denial-of-service you ship to yourself. A 4.7× traffic multiplier during failure is the worst possible time to add load. Backoff and budgets (action item 3) turn clients from amplifiers into dampers.
  • Responders did the right things in the wrong order because the system hid the right order. With a working deploy marker and a latency alert, the 14:20 detour into the wrong valley likely never happens. The fixes target the instruments, not the people who flew with them.
