Trigger — the search query regression (deployed 14:02)

What changed. PR #8841 upgraded invoice search from prefix matching (“starts with”) to contains matching (“anywhere in the number”) — a long-requested feature. Two things changed at once in the ORM layer:

• The pattern gained a leading wildcard: LIKE $1 || '%' became LIKE '%' || $1 || '%'. A B-tree index can serve a prefix (it's a range scan), but a leading wildcard gives it nothing to anchor on — Postgres cannot use the index at all.

• Org scoping moved out of the SQL into application-side post-filtering, so the query ran unscoped across all organizations. Results were identical after filtering — which is exactly why the change passed review and its behavioral tests.

The planner's only remaining option was a sequential scan of the entire invoices table for every search.

Evidence — representative query plans:

Before: Index Only Scan using idx_invoices_org_number (cost=0.56..8.44)
→ mean 0.4 ms, p99 2.1 ms (the index covered the query — the table itself was never touched)

After: Seq Scan on invoices (cost=0.00..6,214,891)
Filter: invoice_number ~~ '%…%'

→ actual time 24,000–31,000 ms per query
(274.2 M live rows + 38.1 M dead ≈ 213 GB of heap)

Why canary didn't catch it. The canary's invoices table holds ~2 M rows; the same scan there takes ~180 ms — slow, but under every alert threshold. The regression is invisible until it meets production-scale data.

Two mercies kept it from being worse: Postgres let concurrent scans share one pass over the table, and the heap was partially cached. Otherwise, scans would have taken minutes, not tens of seconds.

Amplifier 1 — autovacuum debt from the June 22 backfill

What happened. Two days before the incident, a backfill populated the new tax_region column on 38.1 M historical invoices. In Postgres, each UPDATE leaves the old row version behind as a dead tuple — so that one job created 38.1 M dead tuples, growing the table from 190 GB to 213 GB (+12%); 12.2% of all tuples were bloat.

Why cleanup never ran. Autovacuum triggers at 20% of live rows — on a 274.2 M-row table, that's ≈54.9 M dead tuples. The backfill's 38.1 M fell short, so autovacuum — correctly, by its configuration — did nothing. The default is sized for small tables; on very large ones it permits tens of gigabytes of bloat. Statistics were likewise never refreshed.

Why it mattered. Alone, the bloat was harmless — index-backed queries skip dead tuples cheaply, and nothing scans this table in normal operation. But once every search became a full-table scan, each scan read 312.3 M tuples instead of 274.2 M — ~23 GB of extra heap. That pushed scan times from an estimated ~26 s (bloat-free) to the observed 24–31 s: straddling the 30-second statement timeout.

During the incident, ~57% of search queries were killed by the timeout, surfacing to clients as 5xx errors. Bloat converted “slow” into “failing” — and a killed query still held its pooled connection for the full 30 seconds first.

Amplifier 2 — pool exhaustion, multiplied by a retry storm

The arithmetic of exhaustion. The pgbouncer pool to the primary holds 80 connections, shared by invoice reads, writes, PDF rendering, and webhook dispatch. Pre-incident, search used a negligible share (1.2 searches/s × 0.4 ms). After the deploy, each search held a connection for up to 30 s:

• 14:07–15:00 — organic search traffic alone (≈1.2/s × ~28 s) occupied ~34 of 80 connections: heavily degraded, but the pool held.

• Around 15:00 the day's largest cohort of hourly invoice-generation jobs landed, and retries multiplied search arrivals to ≈5.6/s — demand of ≈160+ concurrent connections against a pool of 80. Saturation at 15:12. From then on, every endpoint sharing the pool queued behind 30-second scans — invoice creation, PDF rendering, webhook dispatch. The blast radius stopped being “search”. The client wait queue peaked at 1,437 at 15:58.

The retry storm. SDKs and internal clients retried 5xx responses on a fixed 1-second interval, 3 attempts, no jitter, no retry budget. Under sustained failure that is traffic multiplication: gateway inbound rose from 3,200 rpm baseline to 15,020 rpm at peak — 4.7× — with visible 1-second oscillation as retry waves arrived in phase. The retries queued on the same exhausted pool, so the extra load produced zero extra successes; it only kept the pool pinned for nearly two hours (15:12–17:18).

Why the pool bump backfired. At 16:20 the pool was raised 80 → 200 as an experiment. That admitted more concurrent scans to the database, worsening I/O contention and write latency; it was reverted at 16:31. The pool size wasn't the problem — the 30-second connection holds were.

Remove any one leg — what would June 24 have looked like?

The review's central finding: there is no single root cause. Each leg was necessary; only together were they sufficient. Flip a leg off to see the counterfactual — it's why the fix plan targets all three, not just the trigger.

Query regression the trigger — leading-wildcard, unscoped search

Without the query change: nothing happens at all. The bloat and the retry behavior stay what they had been for months — latent, individually harmless risks.

Autovacuum debt amplifier — 38.1 M dead tuples from the Jun 22 backfill

Without the bloat: scans come in around an estimated ~26 s — painfully slow but mostly under the 30 s timeout, so searches succeed. Retries stay rare, the pool holds at ≈34 of 80 connections: a one-endpoint degradation, not an outage.

Jitterless retries amplifier — fixed 1 s interval, 3 attempts, no budget

Without the retry storm: demand stays near organic levels and the pool holds through the 15:00 spike of scheduled invoice jobs. Search suffers; the rest of the platform stands.

The 68-minute detour

68 minutes elapsed between the first alert (14:11) and the correct diagnosis (15:19) — 50 of them (14:20–15:10) actively pursuing a network hypothesis. Detection and diagnosis lag cost more minutes than any technical failure that day. Four systemic gaps, no individual errors:

Alerts watched error rate, not latency

The only firing signal was “gateway 5xx > 2% over 5 min”. Search p99 exploded at 14:07 — four minutes before that alert — but no latency SLO alert existed for the invoicing endpoint group (checkout had one; invoicing did not).

Dashboards sampled 1 in 100 requests

At 1.2 searches/s, that's ~0.7 samples per minute — the search-latency panel looked like sparse noise, not a cliff, exactly when it mattered.

The deploy marker was broken

The dashboard integration that annotates deploys had been broken since June 10 (a known ticket). The 14:02 deploy was invisible on every graph responders looked at — removing the single strongest hint.

A plausible distractor arrived on cue

The cloud provider's 14:05 packet-loss advisory, plus gateway 504s that look like network timeouts, anchored the team. Given the missing deploy marker, the network theory was the most reasonable available explanation.

In retrospect, pg_stat_activity would have shown rows of identical sequential scans as early as ~14:15. Nothing routed responders toward the database until the pool queue became impossible to miss at 15:15. The review's stance is blameless: the responders did the right things in the wrong order because the system hid the right order — with a working deploy marker and a latency alert, the 14:20 detour likely never happens. The fixes target the instruments, not the people who flew with them.

Why rollback failed — and what worked instead

Release 2026-06-24.3 bundled migration 0412, which dropped idx_invoices_org_number — the covering index the old query depended on. The rationale was reasonable at review time: the new query no longer used it, and it cost 31 GB plus write overhead. In practice, that made the deploy irreversible:

✕ Code rollback

Restores the old prefix query — which now also has no index, and would seq-scan just the same. Abandoned 15:40.

✕ Rebuild the index

≈6 hours on 274 M rows. Started at 16:05 as insurance; finished 21:48 — well after incident close.

✕ Roll forward with a fix

Estimated 50+ minutes of build and deploy under storm conditions, with its own risk.

✕ Bigger pool

Tried 16:20 (80 → 200): more concurrent scans, worse I/O contention and write latency. Reverted 16:31.

✓ Feature flag

Contains-search had shipped behind invoice_search_contains. After staging verified (16:35) that the exact-match fallback used the untouched unique index, the flag went to 0% at 17:05. Errors below 3% within ten minutes. The flip itself took 90 seconds once approved.

The remaining recovery was cleanup. A manual VACUUM (17:12–17:50) removed the 38.1 M dead tuples and refreshed statistics (disk space marked reusable, not returned — a pg_repack is scheduled separately), and buffer caches evicted by hours of 200-GB scans re-warmed. All SLOs were met from 17:53; the incident closed at 18:19. Search ran in degraded, exact-match-only mode until the index rebuild completed that evening; full contains search — with the fixed, org-scoped query — was re-enabled June 26.

What went well

Feature-flag discipline paid for itself. Mitigation required no build, no deploy, no schema change. On June 24, flags — not the deploy pipeline — were the working safety net.

No data was lost. Invoice writes ride a durable queue; all 37,400 delayed webhooks were delivered by 19:05, and no customer record was dropped or corrupted. The blast radius was availability, not integrity.

The lessons behind the plan

  • Latent conditions are part of the system. The bloat and the retry configuration were known-shaped risks, individually harmless for months. Outages of this class are conjunctions — reviews that stop at the trigger fix one third of the problem.
  • Alert on what the customer feels. Customers felt latency at 14:07; the first signal was an error-rate threshold at 14:11, and the dashboards couldn't show the latency cliff at all.
  • Treat irreversible changes as a category. The engineers who bundled migration 0412 followed existing norms; the norms were wrong. Schema changes that foreclose rollback need their own release policy, not more reviewer vigilance.
  • Retries without jitter are a denial-of-service you ship to yourself. A 4.7× traffic multiplier during failure is the worst possible time to add load.
