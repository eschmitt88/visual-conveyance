INC-1194: the June 24 partial API outage, and the three failures behind it

Post-incident analysis · SEV-1 · 2026-06-24 14:02–18:19 UTC (4 h 17 m) · Final, published 2026-07-01 · Blameless review

On June 24 a rewritten search query combined with two pre-existing latent weaknesses — vacuum debt from a bulk backfill two days earlier and jitterless client retries — to turn a one-endpoint regression into a 4-hour-17-minute platform-wide partial outage. There is no single root cause: remove any one of the three legs and June 24 shrinks to a degradation. At peak, 31.2% of API requests were failing; 2,312 customer organizations (24.4% of the 9,480 active that day) were affected, and we expect roughly $41,300 in SLA credits. No data was lost.

Two other facts shape the story. Detection and diagnosis lag dominated the timeline: 68 minutes passed between the first alert (14:11) and the correct diagnosis (15:19), most of it spent on a wrong network hypothesis, because we alerted on error rate rather than latency. And rollback was impossible — the release's migration had irreversibly dropped the old index (rebuild: ~6 hours) — so a feature flag, not the deploy pipeline, stopped the bleeding at 17:05. The remediation plan is layered and scheduled: three P0 items land in Q3 2026, one per leg of the failure.

Why it happened: a trigger and two amplifiers

Cause 1 — the trigger: a search query that abandoned its index

PR #8841 (released at 14:02) upgraded invoice search from "starts with" to "contains" matching. Two things changed at once: the SQL pattern gained a leading wildcard (LIKE '%…%'), and org scoping moved out of the SQL into application-side filtering, so the query ran unscoped across all organizations. Results were identical after filtering, which is why the change passed review and its tests.

Gloss: a B-tree index can serve a "starts with" pattern (it's a range scan), but a leading wildcard gives the index nothing to anchor on — Postgres can't use it at all. The only remaining plan is a sequential scan: read the entire table for every query.

Every search became a sequential scan of the 274-million-row invoices table. Captured query plans show the before/after: an index-only scan at mean 0.4 ms (p99 2.1 ms) became a seq scan taking 24–31 seconds per query. Two mercies kept it from being worse: Postgres let concurrent scans share one pass over the table, and the heap was partially cached — otherwise scans would have taken minutes. Canary didn't catch it because the canary table holds ~2 M rows; the same scan there takes ~180 ms, under every alert threshold. The regression is invisible until it meets production-scale data.

Cause 2 — first amplifier: autovacuum debt turned "slow" into "failing"

Gloss: in Postgres, an UPDATE writes a new row version and leaves the old one behind as a "dead tuple." A background process, autovacuum, reclaims them; until it runs, the table is physically larger ("bloat") and every full-table scan pays to read the corpses.

A June 22 backfill of the tax_region column left 38.1 M dead tuples in invoices — 12.2% bloat, growing the heap from 190 GB to 213 GB. Autovacuum never ran, and by its configuration it was correct not to: the default threshold on a table this size is ≈54.9 M dead tuples, and 38.1 M fell short. The default is sized for small tables; on very large ones it permits tens of gigabytes of bloat.

Alone, the bloat was harmless — nothing scans this table in normal operation. But once every search became a full-table scan, the extra ~23 GB pushed scan times from an estimated ~26 s (bloat-free) to the observed 24–31 s — straddling the 30-second statement timeout. About 57% of search queries were killed by the timeout and surfaced as 5xx errors. And a killed query still held its database connection for the full 30 seconds first.

Cause 3 — second amplifier: pool exhaustion fed by a retry storm

Gloss: requests don't open their own Postgres connections; they borrow from a fixed pool of 80 server connections managed by pgbouncer, shared by invoice reads, writes, PDF rendering, and webhook dispatch. When all are busy, new requests queue.

Pre-incident, search used a negligible share of the pool (1.2 searches/s × 0.4 ms). After the deploy, each search held a connection for up to 30 s. Organic traffic alone occupied ~34 of 80 connections — degraded but surviving. Around 15:00, the day's largest cohort of hourly invoice-generation jobs landed, and retries multiplied search arrivals to ≈5.6/s: demand of 160+ concurrent connections against a pool of 80. Saturation at 15:12. From then on every endpoint sharing the pool queued behind 30-second scans — invoice creation, PDF rendering, webhook dispatch. The blast radius stopped being "search."

The retries were the multiplier: SDKs and internal clients retried 5xx on a fixed 1-second interval, 3 attempts, no jitter, no budget. Gateway inbound rose from a 3,200 rpm baseline to 15,020 rpm at peak — 4.7× — with retry waves arriving in phase. Those retries queued on the same exhausted pool, so the extra load produced zero extra successes; it only kept the pool pinned (client wait queue peaked at 1,437 at 15:58) for nearly two hours. An emergency pool bump to 200 at 16:20 backfired — more concurrent scans worsened write latency — and was reverted at 16:31. The pool size was not the problem; the 30-second holds were.

The chain, end to end

  1. The deploy made every search a 24–31 s full-table scan (trigger).
  2. Backfill bloat pushed scans past the 30 s timeout: slow queries became failed queries (amplifier 1).
  3. Failures triggered jitterless retries, multiplying arrivals 4.7× (amplifier 2).
  4. Multiplied 30-second connection holds exhausted the shared pool, extending failure to every endpoint behind it.
  5. Those endpoints' failures also retried, sustaining the storm until the flag-off at 17:05 removed the scans.

Remove any one leg and the day looks different. Without the bloat, searches are painfully slow but mostly succeed, retries stay rare, and the pool holds — a one-endpoint degradation. Without the retry storm, demand stays near organic levels and the pool survives the 15:00 spike. Without the query change, nothing happens at all. That is why the remediation targets all three legs, not just the trigger.

Why diagnosis took 68 minutes

The gaps were systemic, not human. We alerted on error rate, not latency: search p99 exploded at 14:07, four minutes before any alert, but no latency SLO alert existed for the invoicing endpoint group (checkout had one; invoicing did not). Latency dashboards sampled 1 in 100 requests — at 1.2 searches/s that is ~0.7 samples a minute, so the latency cliff looked like sparse noise. The deploy marker on dashboards had been broken since June 10, so the 14:02 deploy was invisible on every graph responders looked at. And a plausible distractor arrived on cue: the provider's 14:05 advisory plus gateway 504s that look like network timeouts. Given what responders could see, the network theory was the most reasonable available explanation. In retrospect, pg_stat_activity would have shown the identical seq scans as early as ~14:15 — but nothing routed anyone toward the database until the pool queue became impossible to miss at 15:15.

Why rollback failed — and what worked instead

The release bundled migration 0412, which dropped idx_invoices_org_number — the covering index the old query depended on. (A covering index holds all the columns a query needs, so the table itself is never touched.) The rationale was reasonable at review time: the new query no longer used it, and it cost 31 GB plus write overhead. But it made the deploy irreversible in practice: rolling back the code would restore the old query with no index to serve it — it would seq-scan just the same. Rebuilding the index on 274 M rows takes ~6 hours (started at 16:05 as insurance; it finished at 21:48, after incident close), and rolling forward with a query fix meant 50+ minutes of build/deploy under storm conditions.

What worked was the feature flag. The contains-search path had shipped behind invoice_search_contains. After staging verification that the exact-match fallback used an untouched unique index, the flag went to 0% at 17:05; the flip itself took 90 seconds once approved. Search ran in degraded, exact-match-only mode until the index rebuild finished that evening; full contains search returned (with the fixed, org-scoped query) on June 26.

What went well

  • Feature-flag discipline paid for itself. Mitigation needed no build, no deploy, no schema change. On June 24, flags — not the deploy pipeline — were the working safety net.
  • No data was lost. All 37,400 delayed webhooks were delivered by 19:05; no customer record was dropped or corrupted. The blast radius was availability, not integrity.

Lessons

  • Latent conditions are part of the system. The bloat and the retry configuration were known-shaped risks, individually harmless for months. Outages of this class are conjunctions; a review that stops at the trigger fixes one third of the problem.
  • Alert on what the customer feels. Customers felt latency at 14:07; our first signal was an error-rate threshold at 14:11, and our dashboards couldn't show the cliff at all. Detection gaps cost more minutes than any technical failure on June 24.
  • Treat irreversible changes as a category. The engineers who bundled migration 0412 followed our existing norms; the norms were wrong. Schema changes that foreclose rollback need their own release policy, not more reviewer vigilance.
  • Retries without jitter are a denial-of-service you ship to yourself. A 4.7× traffic multiplier during failure is the worst possible time to add load. Backoff and budgets turn clients from amplifiers into dampers.
  • The responders did the right things in the wrong order because the system hid the right order. With a working deploy marker and a latency alert, the 14:20 network detour likely never happens. We are fixing the instruments, not second-guessing the people who flew with them.
