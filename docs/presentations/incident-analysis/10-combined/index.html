No single root cause

A bad deploy was only the trigger. Two pre-existing weaknesses — table bloat from a June 22 backfill and jitterless client retries — turned a one-endpoint regression into a platform-wide outage.

68 minutes lost to diagnosis

First alert 14:11, correct diagnosis 15:19 — 50 of those minutes went to a wrong network theory. Alerts watched error rate, not latency, and latency dashboards sampled 1 in 100 requests.

Rollback was impossible

The release's migration had irreversibly dropped the old index (rebuild: ~6 h). A feature flag — not the deploy pipeline — stopped the bleeding at 17:05.

Fixes are scheduled, not aspirational

Three P0 items land in Q3 2026 — latency SLO alerts, pool-saturation alerting, retries with backoff and jitter — each targeting a different leg of the failure.

Trigger — shipped 14:02

Search rewrite abandoned its index

PR #8841 turned invoice search into a leading-wildcard LIKE that no index can serve: every search scanned all 274 M rows. 0.4 ms became 24–31 s.

Latent weakness 1 — since Jun 22

Autovacuum debt: 38.1 M dead tuples

A backfill left 12.2% bloat that autovacuum's default threshold never cleaned. The extra heap pushed scans past the 30 s timeout — slow queries became errors.

Latent weakness 2 — in every client

Retries with no jitter or budget

Clients retried failures every 1 s, multiplying traffic 4.7×. 30-second connection holds saturated the shared 80-connection pool at 15:12 — taking down every endpoint behind it.

All three together: 4 h 17 m platform-wide partial outage

Search failures spread through the shared connection pool to invoice creation, PDF rendering, and webhook dispatch. Peak error rate 31.2%.

Remove any one leg and the outage shrinks to a degradation — which is why the remediation targets all three, not just the trigger. Details and evidence below.

Why the error rate peaked at 31.2% rather than higher: reporting endpoints served by read replicas, and the separately pooled auth service, stayed healthy throughout.

The three causes, with evidence

Each leg was independently harmless — or nearly so. The outage is their conjunction.

Trigger

1 · The search query regression

PR #8841 upgraded invoice search from "starts with" to "contains" — a long-requested feature. Two things changed at once:

  • The pattern gained a leading wildcard: LIKE '%' || $1 || '%'.
  • Org scoping moved out of SQL into app-side post-filtering, so the query ran unscoped across all organizations.
Why that's catastrophic: a B-tree index is like a sorted phone book — great for "starts with", useless for "contains anywhere". With the org filter gone too, Postgres's only option was reading the entire 274.2 M-row table for every search.
Before: index-only scan, mean 0.4 ms, p99 2.1 ms.
After: sequential scan, 24,000–31,000 ms per query, filtering out 312,344,806 rows each time.

Results were identical after app-side filtering, which is why the change passed review and its behavioral tests. Canary was green too: the canary table holds ~2 M rows, where the same scan takes ~180 ms — under every alert threshold. The regression was invisible until it met production-scale data.

Amplifier 1 — made slow into failing

2 · Autovacuum debt from the June 22 backfill

Gloss: in Postgres, an UPDATE doesn't overwrite a row — it writes a new version and leaves the old one behind as a "dead tuple". A background process, autovacuum, cleans these up. Until it runs, the table is physically larger ("bloat"), and any full-table scan pays to read the corpses.

A June 22 backfill of the new tax_region column updated 38.1 M rows, creating 38.1 M dead tuples and growing the table from 190 GB to 213 GB (12.2% bloat).

Autovacuum never ran — correctly, by its configuration. Its default threshold on a 274.2 M-row table is ≈54.9 M dead tuples; 38.1 M fell short. The default is sized for small tables; on huge ones it permits tens of gigabytes of bloat.

Bloat-free, scans would have taken an estimated ~26 s. With bloat: the observed 24–31 s — straddling the 30-second statement timeout. ~57% of search queries were killed by the timeout and surfaced as 5xx errors. A killed query still held its pooled connection for the full 30 s first.

Alone, this bloat was harmless: index-backed queries skip dead tuples cheaply, and nothing normally scans this table.

Amplifier 2 — spread it platform-wide

3 · Pool exhaustion and the retry storm

Gloss: requests don't open their own database connections; they borrow from a fixed pool of 80 managed by pgbouncer, shared by invoice reads, writes, PDF rendering, and webhook dispatch. When all 80 are busy, new requests queue.

After the deploy, each search held a connection for up to 30 s:

14:07–15:00 · organic search traffic alone: ~34 of 80 connections — degraded, but the pool held
~15:00 · hourly invoice-generation jobs land + retries push search arrivals ≈1.2/s → ≈5.6/s: demand ≈160+ vs 80 — saturation at 15:12

The retry storm. SDKs and internal clients retried 5xx on a fixed 1-second interval, 3 attempts, no jitter, no retry budget. Under sustained failure that is traffic multiplication: gateway inbound rose 3,200 → 15,020 rpm (4.7×), with retry waves arriving in phase. Retries queued on the same exhausted pool — extra load, zero extra successes — keeping the wait queue full for nearly two hours (peak 1,437 waiting clients at 15:58).

Why the 16:20 pool bump backfired: raising the pool 80 → 200 admitted more concurrent scans, worsening I/O contention and write latency; reverted at 16:31. Pool size wasn't the problem — the 30-second holds were.

How they combined

The chain, in order — then what happens if you remove one leg.

  1. The deploy made every search a 24–31 s full-table scan (cause 1 — the trigger).
  2. Backfill bloat pushed those scans past the 30 s statement timeout: slow queries became failed queries (cause 2 — first amplifier).
  3. Failures triggered jitterless retries, multiplying search arrivals 4.7× (cause 3a — second amplifier).
  4. Multiplied 30-second connection holds exhausted the shared pool of 80, extending the failure to every endpoint behind it (cause 3b).
  5. Those endpoints' failures retried too, sustaining the storm until the flag-off at 17:05 removed the scans entirely.

Remove one leg — what changes?

No autovacuum debt

No retry storm

Why diagnosis took 68 minutes — four systemic gaps

Alerts watched error rate, not latency

The only firing signal was "gateway 5xx > 2% over 5 min" (14:11). Search p99 had exploded at 14:07 — four minutes earlier — but no latency SLO alert existed for the invoicing endpoint group (checkout had one; invoicing did not).

Latency dashboards sampled 1 in 100 requests

At 1.2 searches/s that is ~0.7 samples per minute — the search-latency panel looked like sparse noise, not a cliff, exactly when it mattered.

The deploy marker was broken

The dashboard integration that annotates deploys had been broken since June 10 (a known ticket). The 14:02 deploy was invisible on every graph responders looked at — removing the single strongest hint.

A plausible distractor arrived on cue

A cloud-provider "elevated packet loss" advisory at 14:05, plus gateway 504s that look like network timeouts, anchored a network hypothesis for 50 minutes (14:20–15:10) — the most reasonable available explanation, given the missing deploy marker.

In retrospect, pg_stat_activity (Postgres's live query view) would have shown rows of identical sequential scans as early as ~14:15. Nothing routed responders toward the database until the pool queue became impossible to miss at 15:15.

Why rollback failed — and what actually worked

The deploy was irreversible in practice; the feature flag was the working safety net.

Release 2026-06-24.3 bundled migration 0412, which dropped idx_invoices_org_number — the covering index the old query depended on. (A covering index holds every column a query needs, so the query never touches the table itself — that's how old searches ran in 0.4 ms.) The rationale was reasonable at review time: the new query no longer used it, and it cost 31 GB and write overhead. The consequences:

  • Code rollback wouldn't help — the restored prefix query would also have no index, and would seq-scan just the same. Attempted and abandoned at 15:40.
  • Recreating the index takes ~6 hours on 274 M rows. It was started at 16:05 as insurance and finished at 21:48 — well after incident close.
  • Rolling forward with a query fix was estimated at 50+ minutes of build/deploy under storm conditions, with its own risk.

What worked: the contains-search path had shipped behind the feature flag invoice_search_contains. After staging verification (16:35) that the exact-match fallback used the untouched unique index, the flag was set to 0% at 17:05. Errors fell below 3% within ten minutes; the connection wait queue fully drained by 17:18. A manual VACUUM (17:12–17:50) removed the 38.1 M dead tuples and refreshed statistics, caches re-warmed, and all SLOs were met from 17:53. The incident closed at 18:19 after 26 minutes of stable metrics.

Search ran in degraded, exact-match-only mode until the index rebuild completed that evening; full contains search — with the fixed, org-scoped query — was re-enabled June 26. The VACUUM marked disk space reusable rather than returning it; a pg_repack (physical table compaction) is scheduled separately.

What went well

Feature-flag discipline paid for itself

Because the new query path shipped behind a flag, mitigation required no build, no deploy, no schema change — the flip took 90 seconds once approved. On June 24, flags, not the deploy pipeline, were the working safety net.

No data was lost

Invoice writes ride a durable queue and webhooks are queued with replay: all 37,400 delayed webhooks were delivered by 19:05, and no customer record was dropped or corrupted. The blast radius was availability, not integrity.

Lessons

  • Latent conditions are part of the system. The bloat and the retry configuration were known-shaped risks, individually harmless for months. Outages of this class are conjunctions; reviews that stop at the trigger fix one third of the problem.
  • Alert on what the customer feels. Customers felt latency at 14:07; the first signal was an error-rate threshold at 14:11, and the dashboards couldn't show the latency cliff at all. Detection gaps cost more minutes than any technical failure on June 24.
  • Treat irreversible changes as a category. The engineers who bundled migration 0412 followed existing norms; the norms were wrong. Schema changes that foreclose rollback need their own release policy (action item 5), not more reviewer vigilance.
  • Retries without jitter are a denial-of-service you ship to yourself. A 4.7× traffic multiplier during failure is the worst possible time to add load. Backoff and budgets (action item 3) turn clients from amplifiers into dampers.
  • The responders did the right things in the wrong order because the system hid the right order. With a working deploy marker and a latency alert, the 14:20 network detour likely never happens. The fixes target the instruments, not the people who flew with them.

Review posture, per the incident review group: blameless. This analysis names systems, tools, and process gaps, not people — every decision described was reasonable given what responders could see at the time.
