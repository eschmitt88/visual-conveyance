Diffusion models and their variants

How modern image generators actually work — and why so many versions exist

A diffusion model generates images by learning to run a destruction process backwards: a fixed procedure gradually turns real images into pure random noise, a neural network is trained to undo that noising one small step at a time, and every generated image begins as nothing but fresh random noise — never a stored photo. Naive sampling needs ~1000 sequential network passes, so nearly every major variant — DDIM, ODE solvers, latent diffusion, distillation, flow matching — exists to make generation cheaper: faster samplers cut ~1000 steps to 20–50, then 10–20, then 1–4; latent diffusion runs the whole process in a compressed space with roughly 48× fewer values; and classifier-free guidance (the "CFG scale," commonly ≈7.5) steers each step toward your prompt at the cost of diversity and doubled compute.

Destroy carefully, learn to rebuild

A diffusion model is defined by two processes running in opposite directions. The forward (noising) process is fixed and involves no learning: take a real image, add a small amount of Gaussian noise, and repeat — about T = 1000 steps in the original DDPM formulation, following a predefined noise schedule (the recipe for how much noise each step adds). The destruction is gradual: at step 200 the image is slightly grainy, at step 600 only coarse shapes survive, at step 1000 nothing survives — the result is statistically indistinguishable from pure static.

The reverse (denoising) process is what gets learned. A neural network is trained to undo one small step: given a noisy image and its step number t (the "timestep"), estimate a slightly less noisy version. Removing all the noise in one jump would be nearly impossible — countless clean images are consistent with a very noisy input — but removing a little is a tractable prediction, and chaining many small steps is what makes the whole thing work.

Generation then means: sample a fresh image of pure random noise and apply the learned denoising step repeatedly — t = 1000, 999, 998, … down to 0. Structure emerges gradually: blobs of tone, then coarse layout, then object shapes, then fine texture. The output is a new image that never existed anywhere; the only inputs were random numbers and the network's learned weights.

Two things people get wrong. Generation starts from pure noise, not from a photograph — the model is not "cleaning up" any real image (except in explicit image-to-image modes where a user supplies one). And the training images are not stored in the model: a network of a few billion parameters trained on billions of images cannot keep copies; it stores a statistical model of what plausible images look like at every noise level. (One honest nuance: rare near-duplication of training images has been demonstrated, essentially for images repeated many times in training — a real but exceptional memorization failure, not the mechanism.)

What the network predicts, and how it is trained

"The network denoises" hides a design choice. Three output targets are common and mathematically interchangeable — simple algebra converts one to another: noise prediction (ε), the original DDPM choice and still the most common, where the network predicts the noise that was added; score prediction, the gradient of the log-probability density — the direction in pixel space that makes the image more probable (the predicted noise is just a negatively scaled score); and velocity prediction (v), a defined mixture of image and noise that is numerically better behaved at very high noise levels. In text-to-image models the network also receives a representation of the prompt. Architecturally it is typically a U-Net (a convolutional encoder-decoder with skip connections) in the Stable-Diffusion-1/2 generation, or a diffusion transformer (DiT) in newer systems like SD3 and Flux.

Training is remarkably simple. Draw a real image; draw a random timestep; add the corresponding noise in one shot (a closed-form formula gives the correctly noised image for any t); ask the network to predict the added noise and penalize it with a plain mean-squared error. Over billions of examples the network learns denoising at every level — global composition at high noise, fine texture at low noise. No adversarial game as in GANs, where a generator and discriminator must be kept in delicate balance; that stability is a big reason diffusion displaced GANs for large-scale image generation.

The original sin: sampling is slow

Training looks at one noise level at a time, but sampling must walk the whole chain: naive DDPM sampling runs the full network once per step — ~1000 sequential forward passes per image, unparallelizable because each step depends on the previous one. Even on a fast GPU, a single image took the better part of a minute in 2020. Read everything below as one long answer to a single question: how do we take fewer steps?

DDIM: same model, fewer and deterministic steps

DDIM (2020) was the first big win. Using the same trained network, unchanged, one can define a deterministic sampler: fix the initial noise and you get exactly the same image every time. This sampler tolerates much larger jumps — visiting 20–50 steps instead of all 1000 is the typical practical range, with only modest quality loss. Determinism also makes noise-to-image a well-defined function, so you can invert an image back to its noise (useful for editing) and interpolate between images. When a tool exposes a "steps" slider defaulting to ~30, this is the tradeoff it exposes.

One lens that unifies everything: the score/ODE view

In 2021, score-based generative models were shown to be the same thing as diffusion in a continuous-time limit. In this view the forward process is a stochastic differential equation (SDE) — a continuous random drift toward noise — and reversing it needs exactly one learned quantity: the score. There are two routes back: a random reverse-time SDE (like DDPM) or a fully deterministic probability flow ODE; DDIM is essentially a discretization of that ODE. Once sampling is "just solving an ODE," the numerical-solver toolbox applies: purpose-built higher-order solvers (DPM-Solver and successors) reach good samples in ~10–20 steps with no retraining. This view also explains why so many variants coexist — they are different discretizations, paths, or parameterizations of the same underlying object.

Latent diffusion: do it in compressed space

Pixels are expensive: a 512×512 RGB image is 512 × 512 × 3 = 786,432 numbers, all processed at every step. Latent diffusion (2022) adds a compression stage: a separately trained variational autoencoder (VAE) — an encoder-decoder pair — maps each image into a much smaller latent representation and back. In Stable Diffusion the encoder downsamples 8× in each spatial dimension: 512×512×3 becomes a 64×64 latent with 4 channels = 16,384 numbers — roughly 48× fewer values. The compression is perceptual, not lossless: the reconstruction looks essentially identical, with imperceptible detail discarded.

The entire diffusion process — noising, training, sampling — then runs in latent space: sample random noise at 64×64×4, run the denoising loop there (each step ~48× cheaper), and decode the final clean latent through the VAE decoder in one single pass. This is the change that made Stable-Diffusion-class models run on consumer GPUs, and why they are called "latent."

Costs and division of labor. The compression bottleneck imposes a slight quality ceiling — historically visible in fine text and small faces, much improved in newer VAEs — plus one extra decode pass. And the VAE is only a compressor/decompressor; the diffusion model in the middle is what generates.

Classifier-free guidance: steering with the prompt

Text conditioning feeds the prompt into the network (as embeddings from a text encoder such as CLIP or T5, attended to at each step), but conditioning alone follows prompts only loosely. Classifier-free guidance (CFG) is the fix used by essentially every modern text-to-image system. At training time the prompt is randomly dropped for ~10–20% of examples, so one network learns both prompt-conditioned and unconditional denoising. At sampling time every step runs the network twice — with and without the prompt — and extrapolates:

guided = unconditional + s × (conditional − unconditional)

The difference between the two predictions is "the direction the prompt pulls," and the guidance scale s exaggerates it. s = 1 means no guidance; s ≈ 7.5 is a common default in Stable-Diffusion-class models.

The tradeoff is real. Higher s gives stronger prompt adherence and cleaner, more "typical" images, but reduces diversity — and pushed too far (roughly s > 12–15 in classic SD) produces oversaturated colors and burned-in artifacts. CFG is an adherence-vs-diversity knob, not a quality knob. It also doubles the network evaluations per step, which matters when counting cost.

Distillation and consistency models: 1–4 steps

Fast samplers plateau around 10–20 steps. Going lower requires training a model to take big jumps. Progressive distillation trains a student to match, in one step, what a teacher does in two — then repeats, halving the step count each round (1000 → 500 → … → 4). Consistency models (2023) train the network so that any noisy point on a trajectory maps directly to the same clean endpoint, allowing 1-step generation with 2–4 steps as refinement; latent consistency models (LCMs) bring this to Stable-Diffusion-class latents at ~4 steps. Adversarial distillation (SDXL-Turbo-style) adds a GAN-like critic to sharpen few-step outputs. The tradeoff: distilled few-step models trade some quality and diversity for speed — outputs tend to be slightly softer or less varied than the 20–50-step teacher — and the extra training stage adds pipeline complexity. This is the technology behind "real-time" demos that redraw as you type.

Flow matching and rectified flow: straighter paths

The newest mainstream reformulation asks: why follow the curved paths that Gaussian noising dictates at all? Flow matching trains a network to predict a velocity field that transports noise to data along prescribed paths; rectified flow chooses the simplest — straight lines. A training point is just a linear blend of an image and a noise sample, and the regression target is the constant velocity (their difference) along that line. Solving an ODE along nearly straight paths needs far fewer steps than along curved ones, and the formulation drops diffusion-specific scaffolding (noise schedules, timestep-dependent scalings). Mathematically it is a close sibling of diffusion — with Gaussian paths it recovers diffusion's probability-flow ODE, and the score / noise / velocity quantities remain interconvertible — so read it as a reframing that generalizes, not a rival mechanism. Stable Diffusion 3 and Flux-class models are trained this way, combined with transformer backbones and latent-space operation.

Variants at a glance

FamilyTypical stepsCharacter

Distilled / consistency / LCM1–4Retrained for big jumps; fastest; mild quality & diversity cost

Rules of thumb: below ~10 steps, undistilled models degrade visibly; above ~50, returns diminish rapidly. "More steps" refines the same trajectory rather than searching over more images — and for distilled models, the intended step count is part of the design. When counting cost, the honest metric is NFE (number of function evaluations — how many times the network runs), and CFG doubles it per step.

The takeaway

Diffusion, DDIM, score-based models, and flow matching are not competing technologies but one framework: identical or interconvertible training targets, wrapped in different samplers, paths, and parameterizations. The core mechanism never changes — learn to undo gradual noising, then walk from pure randomness back to an image. Everything else in the variant zoo is the field's answer, refined over five years, to a single engineering question: how few steps can we get away with?
