The Denoising Line: a transit map of diffusion image generators

Every AI-generated image is a trip from pure random static to a finished picture. The variants — DDPM, DDIM, latent diffusion, CFG, flow matching — are just different ways to ride the same railway with fewer stops.

Legend — what maps to what

Station dot = one noise level (timestep t)
Track segment = one run of the neural network (one denoising step)
A colored line = a sampler / model variant making the same trip
A full trip, left → right = generating one image from fresh random noise
Grey outbound line = the fixed noising process (used to make training material)
Shaded tunnel = VAE latent space, ≈48× fewer numbers than pixels
Escalator at right = VAE decoder, one pass back to pixels
Guided service = CFG: two network runs per step, steered by scale s

Station dots are drawn schematically — the printed counts (~1000, 20–50, 10–20, ~20–30, 1–4) are the real ones.

Reading this map

Diffusion models are defined by two processes running in opposite directions. The grey outbound line destroys: it takes a real image and adds a little Gaussian noise (TV-static randomness), over and over, until nothing survives. The colored lines rebuild: a trained neural network undoes that destruction one small step at a time, starting from pure random static at the terminus. Every colored line is the same journey — they differ in how many stops they make and whether the network had to be retrained to skip stops. The whole railway runs underground, in a compressed "latent" space; only the final escalator returns to full-resolution pixels.

GREY LINEOutbound: destruction runs on a fixed timetable

The forward (noising) process involves no learning at all. Take a real image, add a small amount of Gaussian noise, and repeat — in the original DDPM formulation, about T = 1000 steps — following a predefined noise schedule (the recipe for how much noise each step adds). The ride is gradual: at stop 200 the image is slightly grainy, at stop 600 only coarse shapes survive, and by stop 1000 the result is statistically indistinguishable from pure Gaussian noise, like analog TV static.

Learning to drive the return trip

The reverse (denoising) process is what gets learned. A neural network is trained to undo one small segment: given a noisy image and the stop number t (the "timestep"), estimate a slightly less noisy version. Removing all the noise in one jump would be nearly impossible — countless clean images are consistent with a very noisy input — but removing a little noise is a tractable prediction, and chaining many small steps is what makes the whole thing work.

Generation is then a trip on this map: board at the terminus with a fresh image of pure random noise and apply the learned denoising step repeatedly — t = 1000, 999, 998, … down to 0. Structure emerges gradually: first blobs of tone, then coarse layout, then object shapes, finally fine texture. The output is a new image that never existed anywhere; the only inputs were random numbers and the network's learned weights.

Training is a denoising exercise repeated billions of times:

  1. Draw a real image from the training set.
  2. Draw a random timestep t — anywhere from light to total noise.
  3. Add the corresponding noise in one shot: a closed-form formula produces the correctly noised image for any t without simulating the intermediate stops.
  4. Ask the network to predict the noise that was added; penalize it with a simple mean-squared error (average squared difference) between predicted and actual noise.

That is the entire objective — formally a weighted variational bound on the data likelihood, but in practice "guess the noise, MSE". Each example exercises one random noise level, so over billions of examples the network learns denoising at every level: at high noise, global composition ("where could a horizon plausibly go?"); at low noise, fine texture. The objective is stable and scalable — no adversarial game as in GANs, where a generator and discriminator must be kept in delicate balance — and that stability is a big reason diffusion displaced GANs for large-scale image generation.

One ticket, three ways to punch it

"The network denoises" hides a design choice: what exactly does it output? Three conventions coexist, and simple algebra converts between them — they are the same ticket, punched differently:

  • Noise (ε) prediction — predict the noise that was added; subtracting a scaled version recovers a clean-image estimate. The original DDPM choice and still the most common.
  • Score prediction — predict the score, the gradient of the log-probability density: the direction in pixel space that makes the image more probable. The predicted noise is just a negatively scaled score.
  • Velocity (v) prediction — predict a defined mixture of image and noise. Introduced with progressive distillation; numerically better behaved at very high noise.

The rolling stock varies too: typically a U-Net (convolutional encoder–decoder with skip connections) in the Stable-Diffusion-1/2 generation, or a diffusion transformer (DiT) in newer systems like SD3 and Flux. In text-to-image models the network also sees a representation of the prompt (see Guided Service below).

LINE 1DDPM Local: why the trip takes forever

Training looks at one noise level at a time, but sampling must ride the whole line. Naive DDPM sampling runs the full network once per stop — ~1000 sequential forward passes per image — and the stops cannot be parallelized, since each depends on the previous one. Even on a fast GPU, a single image took the better part of a minute in 2020. Nearly every other line on this map exists to attack that cost; read the rest of the network as one long answer to: how do we make fewer stops?

LINE 2DDIM Express: same train, fewer and deterministic stops

DDIM (Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models, 2020) was the first big win, built on two observations:

  • DDPM injects fresh randomness at every stop, but an alternative sampler — using the same trained network, unchanged — can be deterministic: fix the initial noise and you get exactly the same image every time.
  • That deterministic sampler tolerates much larger jumps: visit a subsequence of stops instead of all 1000. 20–50 stops is the typical practical range, with only a modest quality loss.

Determinism has side benefits beyond speed: noise → image becomes a well-defined function, so a generated image can be inverted back to its noise (useful for editing), and interpolating between two noises smoothly interpolates the images. When a tool exposes a "steps" slider defaulting to something like 30, it is exposing exactly this line's tradeoff.

LINE 3The track was continuous all along: the score/ODE view

In 2021, a parallel line of work (score-based generative models) was shown to be the same thing as diffusion in a continuous-time limit — the stations are samples along a continuous track. In this merged view, the field's standard mental model today:

  • The forward process is a stochastic differential equation (SDE) (a continuous random drift of images toward noise).
  • Reversing it needs exactly one learned quantity — the score — and there are two ways back: a reverse-time SDE (random, like DDPM) or a probability flow ODE (an ordinary differential equation — fully deterministic) that produces the same distribution of images. DDIM is essentially a discretization of this ODE.

Why care? Once sampling is "just solving an ODE", the entire toolbox of numerical ODE solvers applies. Purpose-built higher-order solvers (DPM-Solver and successors) get good samples in ~10–20 stops with no retraining. This view also explains in one picture why so many lines coexist: they are different discretizations, different paths, or different parameterizations of the same underlying track.

TUNNELThe Latent Underground: run the railway in compressed space

Pixels are expensive: a 512×512 RGB image is 512 × 512 × 3 = 786,432 numbers, all processed by the network at every one of dozens-to-hundreds of stops. Latent diffusion (2022) digs a tunnel. A separately trained variational autoencoder (VAE) (an encoder–decoder pair) maps each image into a much smaller latent representation and back. In Stable Diffusion the encoder downsamples 8× in each spatial dimension: 512×512×3 becomes a 64×64 latent with 4 channels = 16,384 numbers — roughly 48× fewer values. The compression is perceptual, not lossless: reconstructions look essentially identical, with imperceptible detail discarded.

The entire railway — forward noising, training, sampling, exactly as described above — then runs in this latent space:

  1. Sample random noise in latent space (e.g. 64×64×4) at the terminus.
  2. Ride the learned denoising line there — every stop ~48× cheaper than in pixel space.
  3. Take the escalator: decode the final clean latent through the VAE decoder — one single pass — to get the full-resolution image.

This is the change that made Stable-Diffusion-class models run on consumer GPUs, and it is why the model is called "latent". The costs are minor: a slight quality ceiling from the compression bottleneck (historically visible in fine text and small faces, much improved in newer VAEs) and one extra decode pass. Note the division of labor: the VAE is only a compressor/decompressor — the diffusion model in the tunnel is what generates.

GUIDEDGuided Service: steering every line with the prompt

Text conditioning gets the prompt into the network — as embeddings from a text encoder such as CLIP or T5, attended to at each denoising stop — but conditioning alone tends to produce images that only loosely follow the prompt. Classifier-free guidance (CFG) is the fix used by essentially every modern text-to-image system:

  • At training time, the prompt is randomly dropped (replaced by an empty prompt) for a small fraction of examples — typically ~10–20% — so one network learns both prompt-conditioned and unconditional denoising.
  • At sampling time, every segment is driven twice: once with the prompt, once without. The two predictions are blended by extrapolation:

guided = unconditional + s × (conditional − unconditional)

The difference between the two predictions is "the direction the prompt pulls", and the guidance scale s exaggerates it. s = 1 means no guidance; s ≈ 7.5 is a common default in Stable-Diffusion-class models. The tradeoff is real and visible: higher s gives stronger prompt adherence and cleaner-looking, more "typical" images, but reduces diversity — and pushed too far (roughly s > 12–15 in classic SD) it produces oversaturated colors and burned-in artifacts. CFG also doubles the network evaluations per stop, which matters when counting the fare.

LINE 5The Distilled Shuttle: 1–4 stops

Fast samplers plateau around 10–20 stops. Going lower means training a model to take big jumps rather than cleverly scheduling small ones:

  • Progressive distillation trains a student network to match, in one step, what a teacher does in two — then repeats, halving the stop count each round (1000 → 500 → … → 4).
  • Consistency models (2023) train the network so that any noisy point on a given trajectory maps directly to the same clean endpoint. That self-consistency allows 1-stop generation, with 2–4 stops as a refinement option. Latent consistency models (LCMs) apply this to Stable-Diffusion-class latents, giving ~4-stop generation.
  • Adversarial distillation (SDXL-Turbo-style) adds a GAN-like critic to sharpen few-step outputs.

The tradeoff: distilled few-step models trade some quality and diversity for speed — outputs tend to be slightly softer or less varied than the 20–50-stop teacher — and the extra training stage adds pipeline complexity. This is the technology behind "real-time" generation demos that redraw as you type.

LINE 4The Straightaway: flow matching & rectified flow

The newest mainstream reformulation asks: why follow the curved paths that Gaussian noising dictates at all? Flow matching trains a network to predict a velocity field that transports noise to data along prescribed paths; rectified flow chooses the simplest possible ones — straight lines. A training point is just a linear blend of an image and a noise sample, and the regression target is the constant velocity (their difference) along that line.

Why it matters: solving an ODE along nearly straight track needs far fewer stops than along curved track, and the formulation drops the diffusion-specific scaffolding — noise schedules, timestep-dependent scalings — for a cleaner recipe. Mathematically it is a close sibling of diffusion: with Gaussian paths, flow matching recovers diffusion's probability-flow ODE, and the score / noise / velocity quantities remain interconvertible. Read it as a reframing that generalizes, not a rival mechanism. Stable Diffusion 3 and Flux-class models are trained this way, combined with transformer backbones and latent-space operation.

Lost property: common misconceptions

"It searches a database and collages existing images."
False. No training images are stored or consulted at generation time; a network of a few billion parameters trained on billions of images cannot and does not keep copies. Its weights encode statistics of what plausible images look like at every noise level, and sampling starts from random noise. Nuance the map keeps honest: rare near-duplication of training images has been demonstrated, essentially for images repeated many times in the training set — a real but exceptional memorization failure mode, not the mechanism.
"It denoises a real photo."
False for text-to-image generation, which boards at the Pure-Noise Terminus with purely synthetic static. Image-to-image and inpainting modes do start from a user-supplied image — by partially noising it and denoising back — but that is an explicit variant, not the default mechanism.
"The VAE is the generator."
No; the VAE only compresses and decompresses. The diffusion model operating in latent space does the generating.
"More steps always means better images."
Only up to a point (see the table). Past the workhorse range, extra stops refine imperceptibly — and for distilled models, the intended stop count is part of the design.
"The guidance scale is a 'quality' knob."
It is an adherence-vs-diversity knob with an artifact regime at high values; "better" depends on what you want.
"Diffusion, DDIM, score-based models, and flow matching are competing technologies."
They are one framework — one railway: identical or interconvertible training targets, different samplers, paths, and parameterizations.

Where the map breaks

Every metaphor misleads somewhere; here is where this one would, if pushed:

A train carries its passengers unchanged between places; here the "passenger" is transformed at every segment. The stations are not locations in space but noise levels, and the thing riding the line — the image — is itself what changes. Relatedly, riding more stops does not show you more city: extra steps refine the same trajectory toward one image, never a tour past alternative images. And the stations themselves are an artifact of the schematic — the modern view says the track is a continuous ODE/SDE, and each sampler simply chooses where along it to stop.

Pocket glossary

Timestep (t)
Position along the noising process; high t = more noise.
Noise schedule
The predefined recipe for how much noise each forward step adds.
ε / score / velocity
Three interconvertible choices of network output.
Sampler
The algorithm that turns many network predictions into an image (DDPM, DDIM, DPM-Solver, …).
Latent space
The VAE-compressed representation where latent diffusion operates (e.g. 64×64×4 for a 512×512 image).
CFG scale (s)
Strength of the classifier-free-guidance extrapolation.
Distillation
Training a fast student model to imitate a slow teacher.
NFE (number of function evaluations)
How many times the network runs during sampling — the honest cost metric (CFG doubles it per step).
