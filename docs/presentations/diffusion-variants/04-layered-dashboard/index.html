Diffusion models, explained for non-ML professionals

How modern image generators work — and why so many variants exist

Stable Diffusion, Midjourney-class systems, DALL·E 3 and Flux all work the same way: a fixed process gradually destroys images into noise, a neural network learns to run that destruction backwards, and every generated image is built by denoising pure random noise — never by editing a photo or collaging a database. Most variants exist to answer one question: how do we take fewer steps?

The core mechanism

Three pieces: a destruction process you never train, a network that learns to undo it, and a training recipe that is little more than “guess the noise.”

01

The core idea: destroy carefully, learn to rebuild

t: 1000 → 0

Two processes, opposite directions. A fixed forward process gradually drowns real images in noise; a network is trained to undo that destruction one small step at a time. Generating an image means running the learned undo chain on pure random noise.

Full detail

The forward (noising) process is fixed and involves no learning. Take a real image and add a small amount of Gaussian noise (random static drawn from a bell curve). Repeat, over and over — about T = 1000 steps in the original DDPM formulation — following a predefined noise schedule (the recipe for how much noise each step adds). The process is gradual: at step 200 the image is slightly grainy, at step 600 only coarse shapes survive, and by step 1000 nothing survives — the result is statistically indistinguishable from pure Gaussian noise, like analog TV static.

The reverse (denoising) process is what gets learned. A neural network is trained to undo one small step: given a noisy image and the step number t (the “timestep”), estimate a slightly less noisy version. Removing all the noise in one jump would be nearly impossible — countless clean images are consistent with a very noisy input — but removing a little noise is a tractable prediction problem, and chaining many small denoising steps together is what makes the whole thing work.

Generation: sample a fresh image of pure random noise and apply the learned denoising step repeatedly — t = 1000, 999, 998, … down to 0. Structure emerges gradually: first blobs of tone, then coarse layout, then object shapes, finally fine texture. The output is a new image that never existed anywhere; the only inputs were random numbers and the network’s learned weights.

The two most-misunderstood points: (1) Generation starts from pure noise, not from a photograph — the model is not “cleaning up” any real image, except in specialized image-to-image modes where a user deliberately supplies one. (2) Training images are not stored in the model: a network of a few billion parameters trained on billions of images cannot and does not keep copies. What it stores is a statistical model of what plausible images look like at every noise level.

02

What the network actually predicts

ε · score · v

One quantity, three names. The network’s output can be the added noise (ε), the “score” (the direction that makes the image more probable), or a “velocity” mix of both — mathematically interchangeable choices that differ in training stability and convention.

Full detail

“The network denoises the image” hides a design choice: what exactly does it output? Three parameterizations are common. Simple algebra converts one to another, but they differ in training stability and convention:

  • Noise prediction (ε-prediction). Predict the noise that was added; subtracting a scaled version of that prediction recovers an estimate of the clean image. The original DDPM choice, and still the most common.
  • Score prediction. Predict the score — the gradient of the log-probability density of noisy images (the direction in pixel space that makes the image more probable). The predicted noise is just a negatively scaled score: two descriptions of the same quantity.
  • Velocity prediction (v-prediction). Predict a defined mixture of the image and the noise (“velocity”). Introduced with progressive distillation; numerically better behaved at very high noise levels, and used by several later models.

Whichever target is used, the network sees two inputs — the noisy image and the timestep t — plus, in text-to-image models, a representation of the prompt (card 10). Architecturally it is typically a U-Net (a convolutional encoder-decoder with skip connections) in the Stable-Diffusion-1/2 generation, or a diffusion transformer (DiT) in newer systems like SD3 and Flux.

03

Training: a denoising exercise, repeated billions of times

MSE loss

Training is just: guess the noise. Noise a real image by a random amount, ask the network what was added, penalize the error. No adversarial game, unlike GANs — that stability is a big reason diffusion took over large-scale image generation.

Full detail

Training is remarkably simple compared to older generative approaches:

  1. Draw a real image from the training set.
  2. Draw a random timestep t — anywhere from light to total noise.
  3. Add the corresponding amount of noise in one shot: a closed-form formula gives the correctly noised image for any t directly, without simulating the intermediate steps.
  4. Ask the network to predict the noise that was added; penalize it with a simple mean-squared error (average of squared differences) between predicted and actual noise.

That is the entire objective. (Formally it is a weighted variational bound on the data likelihood — a principled statistical target — but the practical loss really is “guess the noise, MSE.”) Each training example exercises one random noise level, so over billions of examples the network learns denoising at every level: at high noise it learns global composition (“where could a horizon plausibly go?”), at low noise it learns fine texture.

This objective is stable and scalable — no adversarial game as in GANs (generative adversarial networks, where a generator and a discriminator must be kept in delicate balance) — and that stability is a big reason diffusion displaced GANs for large-scale image generation.

The speed problem — and the fixes

Training looks at one noise level at a time; sampling must walk the whole chain. Nearly every major variant is an answer to “how do we take fewer steps?”

04

Why sampling is slow — the original sin

~1000 passes

~1000 sequential network runs per image. Naive DDPM sampling runs the full network once per step, and the steps can’t be parallelized — each depends on the previous one. In 2020 a single image took the better part of a minute on a fast GPU.

Full detail

Training looks at one noise level at a time, but sampling must walk the whole chain. Naive DDPM sampling runs the full network once per step — ~1000 sequential forward passes per image. The steps cannot be parallelized, because each depends on the previous one, so even on a fast GPU a single image took the better part of a minute in 2020.

Nearly every major variant since exists to attack this cost. It helps to read the whole variant zoo — DDIM, ODE solvers, distillation, consistency models, rectified flow — as one long answer to the question: how do we take fewer steps?

05

DDIM: same model, fewer and deterministic steps

20–50 steps

Fewer, deterministic steps — no retraining. DDIM (2020) reuses the same trained network with a deterministic sampler that tolerates big jumps: 20–50 steps is the everyday range. The “steps” slider in image tools exposes exactly this tradeoff.

Full detail

DDIM (Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models, 2020) was the first big win. Two observations:

  • The DDPM sampler injects fresh randomness at every step, but one can define an alternative sampler — using the same trained network, unchanged — that is deterministic: fix the initial noise and you get exactly the same image every time.
  • This deterministic sampler tolerates much larger jumps. Instead of visiting all 1000 timesteps, visit a subsequence — 20–50 steps is the typical practical range — with only a modest quality loss.

Determinism has side benefits beyond speed: the mapping from initial noise to final image becomes a well-defined function, so you can invert a generated image back to its noise (useful for editing) and smoothly interpolate between two images by interpolating their noise. When an image tool exposes a “steps” slider defaulting to something like 30, it is exposing exactly this tradeoff.

06

The score-based / SDE view: one lens that unifies everything

~10–20 steps

It’s all one equation underneath. In 2021, score-based models were shown to be diffusion in continuous time: sampling becomes “just solving an ODE,” so off-the-shelf numerical solvers (DPM-Solver and successors) reach good samples in ~10–20 steps with no retraining.

Full detail

In 2021, a parallel line of work (score-based generative models) was shown to be the same thing as diffusion in a continuous-time limit, and the merged view is the field’s standard mental model today:

  • The forward noising process is a stochastic differential equation (SDE) (a differential equation with built-in randomness — a continuous random drift of images toward noise).
  • Reversing it requires exactly one learned quantity: the score (the gradient of log-density from card 02). With the score in hand there are two ways back: a reverse-time SDE (random, like DDPM) or a probability flow ODE (an ordinary differential equation — no randomness) that is fully deterministic and produces the same distribution of images. DDIM is essentially a discretization of this ODE.

Why a non-ML reader should care: once sampling is “just solving an ODE,” the entire toolbox of numerical ODE solvers applies. Purpose-built higher-order solvers (DPM-Solver and successors) get good samples in ~10–20 steps with no retraining. This view also explains, in one picture, why so many variants coexist: they are different discretizations, different paths, or different parameterizations of the same underlying object.

07

Distillation and consistency models: 1–4 steps

1–4 steps

Train for big jumps. Below ~10–20 steps you must retrain: progressive distillation halves the step count round by round; consistency models map any noisy point straight to the clean endpoint, enabling 1-step generation. This powers “real-time” demos — at a mild quality and diversity cost.

Full detail

Fast samplers plateau around 10–20 steps. Going lower requires training a model to take big jumps rather than cleverly scheduling small ones:

  • Progressive distillation trains a student network to match, in one step, what a teacher does in two — then repeats, halving the step count each round (1000 → 500 → … → 4).
  • Consistency models (2023) train the network so that any noisy point on a given trajectory maps directly to the same clean endpoint. That self-consistency property allows 1-step generation, with 2–4 steps as a refinement option. Latent consistency models (LCMs) apply this to Stable-Diffusion-class latents, giving ~4-step generation.
  • Adversarial distillation (SDXL-Turbo-style) adds a GAN-like critic to sharpen few-step outputs.

CaveatDistilled few-step models trade some quality and diversity for speed — outputs tend to be slightly softer or less varied than the 20–50-step teacher — and the extra training stage adds pipeline complexity.

This is the technology behind “real-time” generation demos that redraw as you type.

08

Flow matching and rectified flow: straighter paths

SD3 · Flux

Straighten the path, take fewer steps. Rectified flow trains the network to move along straight lines from noise to image — a cleaner recipe that needs fewer solver steps. It is a reframing of diffusion, not a rival; Stable Diffusion 3 and Flux-class models are trained this way.

Full detail

The newest mainstream reformulation asks: why follow the curved paths that Gaussian noising dictates at all? Flow matching trains a network to predict a velocity field that transports noise to data along prescribed paths; rectified flow chooses the simplest possible ones — straight lines. A training point is just a linear blend of an image and a noise sample, and the regression target is the constant velocity (their difference) along that line.

Why this matters: solving an ODE along nearly straight paths needs far fewer steps than along curved ones, and the formulation drops the diffusion-specific scaffolding — noise schedules, timestep-dependent scalings — for a cleaner recipe.

Mathematically it is a close sibling of diffusion: with Gaussian paths, flow matching recovers diffusion’s probability-flow ODE, and the score / noise / velocity quantities remain interconvertible. It is best read as a reframing that generalizes, not a rival mechanism. Stable Diffusion 3 and Flux-class models are trained this way, combined with transformer backbones and latent-space operation.

Scale and steering

Two changes made diffusion practical and controllable: run it in a compressed space, and steer it with the prompt at every step.

09

Latent diffusion: do it in compressed space

≈48× fewer values

Diffuse in compressed space. A separately trained VAE squeezes a 512×512×3 image (786,432 numbers) into a 64×64×4 latent (16,384 numbers — roughly 48× fewer); diffusion runs entirely there. This is what made Stable-Diffusion-class models run on consumer GPUs.

Full detail

Everything in the cards above operates directly on pixels, and pixels are expensive: a 512×512 RGB image is 512 × 512 × 3 = 786,432 numbers, and the denoising network must process all of them at every one of dozens-to-hundreds of steps.

Latent diffusion (2022) adds a compression stage. A separately trained variational autoencoder (VAE) (an encoder-decoder pair that compresses and decompresses images) maps each image into a much smaller latent representation and back. In Stable Diffusion the encoder downsamples 8× in each spatial dimension: a 512×512×3 image becomes a 64×64 latent with 4 channels = 16,384 numbers — roughly 48× fewer values. The compression is perceptual, not lossless: the VAE is trained so the reconstruction looks essentially identical, discarding imperceptible detail.

The diffusion process — forward noising, training, and sampling exactly as already described — then runs entirely in this latent space. Generation becomes:

  1. Sample random noise in latent space (e.g. 64×64×4).
  2. Run the learned denoising loop there — every step ~48× cheaper than in pixel space.
  3. Decode the final clean latent through the VAE decoder — one single pass — to obtain the full-resolution image.

This is the change that made Stable-Diffusion-class models run on consumer GPUs, and it is why the model is called “latent.”

CaveatThe costs are minor but real: a slight quality ceiling from the compression bottleneck — historically visible in fine text and small faces, much improved in newer VAEs — plus one extra decode pass.

Note the division of labor: the VAE is only a compressor/decompressor — the diffusion model in the middle is what generates.

10

Classifier-free guidance: steering with the prompt

s ≈ 7.5

Predict twice, exaggerate the difference. Every step runs the network with and without the prompt, then extrapolates: guided = unconditional + s × (conditional − unconditional). The scale s (~7.5 by default) trades diversity and naturalness for prompt adherence — and doubles per-step cost.

Full detail

Text conditioning gets the prompt into the network — as embeddings from a text encoder such as CLIP or T5 (a model that turns text into number vectors), attended to at each denoising step — but conditioning alone tends to produce images that only loosely follow the prompt. Classifier-free guidance (CFG) is the fix used by essentially every modern text-to-image system:

  • At training time, the prompt is randomly dropped (replaced by an empty prompt) for a small fraction of examples — typically ~10–20% — so one network learns both prompt-conditioned and unconditional denoising.
  • At sampling time, every step runs the network twice: once with the prompt, once without. The two predictions are blended by extrapolation:

guided = unconditional + s × (conditional − unconditional)

The difference between the two predictions is “the direction the prompt pulls,” and the guidance scale s exaggerates it. s = 1 means no guidance; s ≈ 7.5 is a common default in Stable-Diffusion-class models.

CaveatThe tradeoff is real and visible: higher s gives stronger prompt adherence and cleaner-looking, more “typical” images, but reduces diversity — and pushed too far (roughly s > 12–15 in classic SD) it produces oversaturated colors and burned-in artifacts. CFG also doubles the network evaluations per step, which matters when counting sampling cost.

Getting it right

The misconceptions worth debunking, and the jargon you’ll meet in tool settings and articles.

11

Common misconceptions

6 myths

Six things people get wrong. It doesn’t collage a database, it doesn’t clean up a real photo, the VAE isn’t the generator, more steps isn’t always better, the guidance scale isn’t a quality knob — and the variants aren’t competing technologies.

Full detail

  • ✕“It searches a database and collages existing images.”

    False. No training images are stored or consulted at generation time; the model’s weights encode statistics, not copies, and sampling starts from random noise.

    NuanceRare near-duplication of training images has been demonstrated — essentially for images repeated many times in the training set. A real but exceptional memorization failure mode, not the mechanism.

  • ✕“It denoises a real photo.”

    False for text-to-image generation, which starts from pure synthetic noise. Image-to-image and inpainting modes do start from a user-supplied image — by partially noising it and denoising back — but that is an explicit variant, not the default mechanism.

  • ✕“The VAE is the generator.”

    No; the VAE only compresses and decompresses. The diffusion model operating in latent space does the generating.

  • ✕“More steps always means better images.”

    Only up to a point. Past the workhorse range (~50 steps), extra steps refine imperceptibly — and for distilled models, the intended step count is part of the design.

  • ✕“The guidance scale is a ‘quality’ knob.”

    It is an adherence-vs-diversity knob with an artifact regime at high values; “better” depends on what you want.

  • ✕“Diffusion, DDIM, score-based models, and flow matching are competing technologies.”

    They are one framework: identical or interconvertible training targets, different samplers, paths, and parameterizations.

Glossary

8 terms

The jargon, one line each — the terms you’ll actually see in tool settings: timestep, noise schedule, sampler, latent space, CFG scale, distillation, NFE.

Full detail
Timestep (t)
Position along the noising process; high t = more noise.
Noise schedule
The predefined recipe for how much noise each forward step adds.
ε / score / velocity
Three interconvertible choices of network output.
Sampler
The algorithm that turns many network predictions into an image (DDPM, DDIM, DPM-Solver, …).
Latent space
The VAE-compressed representation where latent diffusion operates (e.g. 64×64×4 for a 512×512 image).
CFG scale (s)
Strength of classifier-free guidance extrapolation.
Distillation
Training a fast student model to imitate a slow teacher.
NFE (number of function evaluations)
How many times the network runs during sampling — the honest cost metric (CFG doubles it per step).
